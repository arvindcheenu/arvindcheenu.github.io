What people attend to shapes what they find, and in data analysis it disappears the moment it happens. I build systems that reflect attention back: where you have looked, what you passed over, where others looked before you. Seeing your own attention helps you manage it. Seeing someone else’s lets you retrace how they reached a conclusion, question it and build on it. A trail like that lowers the bar for joining an analysis, a step toward opening it to anyone, anytime, anywhere.

My PhD at the Center for Anytime Anywhere Analytics (CA³), Aarhus University, advised by Niklas Elmqvist, develops this as attention-aware analytics on the web and in mixed reality, following attention from the analyst at work to collaborators sharing a space, an observer reviewing the session afterwards and a reader who receives only the findings. Built mostly on open web standards such as WebXR, the systems run in an ordinary browser or headset. The first two, Attention-Aware Visualization and HeedVision, appeared at IEEE VIS and in IEEE TVCG.

It began with my master’s in HCI at the University of Maryland with Joel Chan, where I studied how people notice useful analogies; that work earned two Best Paper Honorable Mentions at ACM Creativity & Cognition. It now runs across ubiquitous analytics, collaborative immersive analytics, context-aware systems and human-centred AI.

As AI systems take on more of the looking for us, the same questions reach them: what did they attend to, and what did they miss? And with us humans in the loop, how would we know if they did? Making attention visible on both sides is how people keep their cognitive autonomy, deciding for themselves where to look, what to trust and what to conclude.

I build interfaces for working with data across screens, headsets and teams. My focus is making that work traceable: recording what people attend to as they analyse and putting it to use, for the person at the screen, for collaborators in the same mixed-reality space, and for whoever picks up the analysis next.

As a PhD fellow at the Center for Anytime Anywhere Analytics, Aarhus University, I write these systems end to end for the web, Meta Quest 3 and HoloLens 2: gaze capture, per-mark visibility tracking, event-logged back ends and the views that replay them. I run the user studies that test them, and two of the systems are published in IEEE TVCG. I am now building heed, an open-source toolkit that brings the same recording to any web page.

Before Aarhus I did a master’s in HCI at the University of Maryland, building a paper-processing pipeline and Elasticsearch-based analogical search as a research assistant, and worked as a UX intern at Tetrate. I work mostly in TypeScript, React, WebXR, Python and Unity. I want to build products that show what has been looked at and what has been missed, by a person, a team or an AI tool, so people can decide what to check before they act.

Publications

In all publications, my name is in bold, and authors who contributed equally are marked with a dagger (†). I follow the convention where the first author is the lead author, and the last author is often the most senior author with a supervisory role in the project. My primary area of publication is computer science, where conference papers are often counted as having equal or higher prominence to journal publications. These conference papers are strictly peer-reviewed with at least three external reviewers and typically have acceptance rates of 30% or lower. Acceptance rates are given for all conference papers (if known); these are specific to each year. IEEE VIS is the leading international conference on visualization, and its full papers appear in IEEE TVCG. ACM CHI is the leading international conference on human-computer interaction. ACM Creativity & Cognition is the ACM conference on creativity research and the tools that support it. ETVIS is the visualization workshop of ACM ETRA, the main conference on eye-tracking research.
